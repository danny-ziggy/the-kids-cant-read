A short history of being sure it was already happening

PreviouslyThree teachers went viral saying their students can't read. Then a number.

Before we get to what is actually happening, a short history of everyone being sure it was happening already.

People have been worried about kids and reading for as long as there have been kids and reading. The worry changes costume every generation. Watch the bottom of each clipping, though. That's where the joke is, and also the point.

Notice a pattern?

Keep scrolling.

Look at the stamps. For most of 230 years, the complaint about kids and reading ran on vibes: a feeling, a bestseller, a sermon about novels. You can't check a vibe.

But since 1971, the federal government has given 13-year-olds a reading test that is built, on purpose, to stay the same, so a score in one decade means the same thing in the next. It's called the NAEP Long-Term Trend assessment. It is boring. Boring is exactly what you want in a ruler.

Here is what the ruler says.

255

1971. Our starting line: the average reading score of American 13-year-olds on the long-term test.

Then, for forty years, a slow, unglamorous climb. Not a boom. More of a shuffle upward, with a few stumbles.

263

2012. The highest score the test has recorded for 13-year-olds.

256

Then it falls. Notice when: it starts in 2012, years before the pandemic. By 2025 the line is back about where it began in 1971, down 7 points from the peak.

NAEP Long-Term Trend. Scores between 1971 and 2008 come from NAEP's published series.

So were the panics right all along? Not quite. They were almost always about everyone, and they treated "can't read" as one thing.

It isn't. "The kids can't read" is three different complaints wearing one coat. Before I name them, try them.

Three kinds of can't: can't sound it out, can't get the meaning, won't stay with it. They get blurred into one panic. They have different causes, they hit different people, and they have different fixes.

Decoding and comprehension, and who's losing them

Previously"Can't read" turned out to mean three things: can't sound it out, can't get the meaning, won't stay with it.

Here's a question that sounds simple. Are American kids worse readers than they used to be?

The honest answer is: which kids? And the best way I know to show you what I mean is to make you stand in a line.

These are 100 twelfth graders, lined up from the weakest reader to the strongest. It's 1992, where NAEP's twelfth-grade reading trend begins, and everyone is standing where 1992 put them. Now drag the year to 2024.

Where would you have stood?

Pick your spot in your senior class. Be honest. Nobody's watching, and nothing leaves this page.

Look at the shape of that. The strongest readers barely moved: in 2024 the 90th percentile was statistically indistinguishable from 1992. The 75th slipped 2 points. The middle fell 8. The 25th percentile fell 15. And the bottom tenth fell 24 points.

The overall average dropped 10 points, to the lowest ever reported for seniors. But an average hides the shape. This isn't a country getting slightly worse at reading. It's a line coming apart.

Two more numbers from the same seniors. Only 35% scored as ready for college-level reading, down from 37% in 2019. And 31% had missed three or more days of school in the previous month, up from 26%. You can't learn to read in a room you're not in.

What "below Basic" means

Not "can't read words." Can't read between them.

NAEP's governing board defines the line carefully. A fourth grader below Basic likely can't identify the reason for a character's action when the story implies it rather than saying it outright. That's the skill you used on Mara. Here's one pitched at nine-year-olds.

Grade 4 · inferenceSample item written for this page. Not a NAEP item.

Ben got a red kite for his birthday. On Sunday he looked out the window at the flags in the park. They hung straight down. Ben left the kite in the closet and took his soccer ball instead.

Why didn't Ben bring his kite?

B. The story never mentions wind. You connected flags hanging straight down to a kite that won't fly. That connection is the whole line between Basic and below it.

Peggy Carr, then commissioner of the federal statistics agency behind NAEP, has cautioned that below Basic doesn't mean a child can't read, and that achievement levels should be interpreted with care. Fair. A local news fact check landed in the same place: most kids aren't illiterate in the strict sense. What they're missing is the step from the words to the meaning.

Why you might not see any of this

If you are reading a long interactive feature about reading, for fun, odds are decent that the kids in your life are standing near the right end of that line. And from there, things look fine, because there, things are fine.

Houston, Texas · revisited

One classroom isn't a country

Back to the clips. After Darius Williams's video spread, students at his school disputed his account to local public radio, and the district pointed out that Wheatley had just earned its first "A" rating from the state. Ms. L's count is her count, of her students, by her method.

So no, one classroom isn't a country. Viral clips are terrible evidence about everyone.

But here's the uncomfortable part. On the narrow question of the bottom of the line, the clips and the national data agree. There really are a lot more teenagers who can't get meaning out of two paragraphs than there were a decade ago. The teachers weren't making it up. They were just standing at one end of the line.

Act TwoDid Not Finish

The habit, and the people losing it, which is most people

PreviouslyThe weakest readers fell furthest. The strongest held still.

Act One was about skill. This one is about something softer and, I think, stranger: not whether people can read, but whether anyone still does it for fun.

Since 1984, NAEP has asked 13-year-olds a simple question: how often do you read for fun on your own time?

Age 13 · reads for fun almost every dayNAEP Long-Term Trend

From 35% to 14%. Meanwhile, the kids who read for fun never or hardly ever went from 22% to 31%. By 2023 the non-readers outnumbered the daily readers more than two to one.

And before you blame teenagers, who are always the easiest people to blame: it isn't only them.

9-year-oldsread for fun almost daily, 2012 → 2025
13-year-oldsalmost daily, 1984 → 2025
Adultsread for pleasure on a given day, 2004 → 2023

The adult number comes from the American Time Use Survey, which asks people what they did yesterday. One small twist: the adults who still read spend slightly more time at it than readers used to. The habit isn't spreading thin. It's retreating to a smaller group of people who really mean it.

In March, a small-town library received a padded envelope with no return address. Inside was a hardcover sea novel, checked out in 1983 and never seen again. The due-date card was still in its paper pocket, stamped in faded purple ink. Tucked between the pages was a ticket stub from a movie theater that closed in 1991.

The librarians did the math first. At the old rate of five cents a day, the fine would have come to nearly eight hundred dollars. The library had stopped charging late fees years ago, so the number was just for fun, and someone posted it on the bulletin board by the front desk, where it drew a small crowd. A reporter from the weekly paper came by to take its picture.

Then came the theories. One regular was sure the borrower had moved overseas and only just come home. Another thought the book had spent decades in a relative's attic and turned up during a cleanout. A third pointed at a coffee ring on the cover and decided it had simply been used as a coaster for forty years, which is a kind of reading, if you're generous. A fourth was certain a guilty grandchild had found it and mailed it back in secret.

The envelope held one more thing, which the librarians found only after shaking it: a folded index card in careful blue pen. It apologized for the delay. It thanked the library for its patience. It enclosed a twenty-dollar bill for the trouble. It said the book had survived four apartments, two marriages and one flood, and had held up better than the borrower's furniture.

On its last line, the card explained the forty years. The borrower had stopped on page 212, and had refused to return the book until finishing it.

Act ThreeSounding It Out

What fixes what, and what nobody knows

PreviouslyThe habit is fading for nearly everyone, and none of the suspects acts alone.

Go back to the cold open for a second. You couldn't read the title, and nothing was wrong with you. You hadn't been taught the code.

Now imagine that, instead of teaching you the code, someone taught you a trick for getting around it. When you hit a word you don't know, look at the picture. Think about what would make sense. Guess.

Illustrative · not a real classroom item

Guess, or sound it out?

A word sits under a picture. You know the picture. You don't know the alphabet. Two ways to handle it:

Pick one. Then try the other.

The guess was reasonable. It made sense, it fit the picture, it was a real word. It was also wrong, and nothing about the guessing strategy could have told you so. Only the letters could.

That trick has a name: three-cueing. For decades, a lot of American classrooms taught beginning readers to work out unfamiliar words from three cues: does it make sense, does it sound right, does it look right. The letters often came last. The most famous curriculum built around this, Lucy Calkins's Units of Study, was in use in nearly a quarter of elementary schools. It was revised in 2022, with more phonics.

The same year, a podcast called Sold a Story laid out how the guessing approach spread and what it cost. The argument is simple and, once you've felt it in an alphabet you don't know, hard to unhear: guessing is what struggling readers already do. Teaching it as a strategy trains the problem.

The alternative isn't new. It's the thing Rudolf Flesch was yelling about in 1955. Phonics: teach the code, sound by sound, explicitly and in order, until it's automatic. And there is a state that tried it at scale.

The catch: the gains fade by eighth grade. Decoding opens the door. It doesn't furnish the room. And the third can't?

So here's where we are. Two of the three can'ts have known fixes, and they're boring in the best way: teach the code, screen early, tutor hard, fill kids' heads with things worth reading about. Some states are doing it. Some of it is working.

The third one is different. Nobody has run the randomized trial on "assign whole books again," or "let kids see adults reading for no reason." Those might work. They're cheap. Nobody knows. The habit has no proven fix.

I'm not going to tell you to read more. You know where the books are. But remember the cold open: the few seconds when the letters meant nothing, and then, one at a time, they did. Somebody taught you that. It took years, and it is the most useful thing anyone ever did for you that you don't remember them doing.

Worth passing on.
