Writing is about 5,000 years old

Bookkeepers in the Mesopotamian city of Uruk pressed the first known writing into clay around 3200 B.C. For nearly all the time since, most people could not read.

3200 B.C.

The first known writing

Each figure: 1 in 10 people worldwide. Source: Our World in Data

Mesopotamian cuneiform, the oldest known writing system, began as a way to track goods. Writing was invented on its own at least four times: in Mesopotamia, Egypt, China and Mesoamerica.

The literacy shares come from Our World in Data's world series: about 12% in 1820, just over 20% in 1900, 42% in 1960, 70% in 1983 and 87% today. There are no world estimates before 1820, so the figures stay unlit until then.

Sources: Our World in Data, World Economic Forum and Statista on the same series, Discover, World History Encyclopedia, Dehaene in Cerebrum.

No part of the brain evolved to read

Writing arrived far too recently for evolution to build the brain a reading region.

Reading takes over a patch of visual cortex that evolved to recognize objects and faces. The neuroscientist Stanislas Dehaene calls the takeover neuronal recycling.

Children pick up speech by listening, but many learn to read only when someone teaches them directly which shapes stand for which sounds. Teaching it that way is called phonics.

The region is called the visual word form area. In everyone who reads, it sits in about the same spot in the left hemisphere, beside areas that respond to faces and objects. A 2020 study by researchers at MIT, with Dehaene, found that the same part of the visual system in monkeys can already tell real words from strings of random letters, which suggests reading builds on machinery that was there first.

“The brain did not evolve for culture,” Dehaene told Scientific American, “but culture evolved to be learnable by the brain.” He notes that the alphabet is only about 4,000 years old.

G. Reid Lyon, who led reading research at the National Institutes of Health, made the case in a 1998 article titled “Why Reading Is Not a Natural Process.” In speech, the separate sounds inside a word run together and are never consciously heard, so no one gets natural practice noticing them.

Sources: Dehaene and Cohen, 2011, MIT News, 2020, Scientific American, Dehaene in Cerebrum, Lyon, 1998.

Socrates worried about writing

In the dialogue, the Egyptian king Thamus tells Theuth, the inventor of letters, that writing “will create forgetfulness in the learners' souls.” The quotations come from Benjamin Jowett's public-domain translations of Plato and, for Augustine, E.B. Pusey's.

PISA 2025 also found that about half of 15-year-olds use AI to learn every week, and that using it for writing went with lower reading scores. That is a correlation, not proof that AI caused the drop.

Sources: Plato, Phaedrus (Project Gutenberg), Augustine, Confessions VI.3, OECD, PISA 2025.

Only the newest alarm comes with half a century of national test scores to check it against.

In the second half of the 1700s, German writers argued over young people's appetite for novels, which critics called Lesesucht: an addiction, a fever or a mania. The historian Henning Wrage calls the debate the first important argument about media harming the young in modern times. Rudolf Flesch's Why Johnny Can't Read made the case for phonics in 1955. A Nation at Risk, a federal report, warned of “a rising tide of mediocrity” in 1983.

The National Assessment of Educational Progress, known as the Nation's Report Card, first tested reading in 1971. Its long-term trend test has used largely the same kinds of questions since.

Sources: Wrage, Monatshefte, History Today, NCES.

How real is it?

Fifty years of 13-year-olds

Since 1971, the federal government has tested 13-year-olds' reading on a scale of 0 to 500. The average has barely moved.

At 256, the average 13-year-old can connect ideas and draw general conclusions from short passages about science, history or literature.

The average hides a widening gap. The strongest 13-year-olds now outscore their 1971 counterparts, while the weakest do no better and have lost the most ground since 2012.

Source: NAEP Long-Term Trend, NCES

The long-term trend test gave 13-year-olds an average of 255 in 1971, a peak of 263 in 2012 and 256 in both 2023 and 2025. In 2004 the test's format changed; the chart uses the revised score, 257.

The score bands come from the test's own descriptions. At 200, students can understand, combine ideas and make inferences from short, uncomplicated passages. At 250, they can search for specific information, connect ideas and generalize about literature, science and social studies. At 300, they can find, understand, summarize and explain relatively complicated material.

The strongest and weakest tenths are the 90th and 10th percentiles. The lines show every published value from 1971 to 1996, then 2020 and 2023; the dashed stretch skips years not plotted here. In 2023 the 10th percentile scored 202, below its 1971 score of 208, and the commissioner of the National Center for Education Statistics, Peggy Carr, said so. From 2012 to 2020, the 10th percentile was the only group whose score changed significantly. In 2025, neither end changed significantly from 2023, and only the highest scorers remained significantly above 1971.

Sources: NCES, 2025 appendix, NCES Digest, 1997, NCES release, 2023, NCES release, 2021, NCES score-level descriptions, CSBA on 2025.

What does it look like?

Sounding it out

In this invented alphabet, each shape stands for a sound, the way letters do for a child just learning them.

Fluent readers know most words on sight, which leaves their attention free for meaning. A reader still sounding out every word spends it on the sounds, and the start of a sentence slips away before the end arrives.

The helper's habit has a name. Three-cueing taught children to work out a word from the picture, the sentence and the first letter instead of sounding it out. In a 2019 Education Week survey, about three in four teachers of kindergarten through second grade said they used it. At least 14 states now bar it.

Two shapes in the key stand for two letters at once, “sh” and “oo,” because each spells a single sound. Phonics teaches those pairs directly.

What happens as they move up?

Grades rise as scores fall

From 2015 to 2024, the average GPA of high-school graduates who took the ACT rose from 3.23 to 3.43, while their average ACT score fell from 21.0 to 19.5.

Source: ACT Research Report R2514, July 2026. Grades are self-reported.

ACT's figures cover graduating classes that took its test. In 2024, 42% of them did not report a GPA, and the number of test takers fell from 2.09 million in 2017 to 1.39 million in 2024, so the group changed over time. ACT sells tests and argues for test requirements. A federal study of real transcripts found the same direction: average GPA rose from 3.00 in 2009 to 3.11 in 2019 while 12th-grade national test scores did not improve.

The parent figures come from a Gallup survey for the nonprofit Learning Heroes of about 2,000 public-school parents. Seventy-nine percent said their child got mostly B's or better, and 64% called report cards an important measure. The fine print

Adults at those levels can read short texts with clearly marked facts but struggle when the facts are spread across a longer one.

Sample task written for this page

Jamie sees a specialist about six times a year.

Plan A$150 a monthSpecialist visit: $60
Plan B$110 a monthSpecialist visit: $20 *

* After the $1,500 annual deductible. Until then, each specialist visit costs $200.

Which plan costs Jamie less in a year?

Plan A$2,160
Plan B$2,520

Plan A: 12 × $150 + 6 × $60 = $2,160. Plan B: 12 × $110 + 6 × $200 = $2,520. Six visits at $200 come to $1,200, under the $1,500 deductible, so the $20 price never applies.

The OECD's lowest levels, Level 1 and below, describe adults who can read short texts and lists with clearly marked information but struggle to compare sources or find facts spread across a longer text. The U.S. share of adults at those levels rose from 19% in 2017 to 28% in 2023. Low health literacy goes with more hospital stays and more emergency care, a 2011 federal review found.
