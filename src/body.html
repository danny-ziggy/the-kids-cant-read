The Boy Who Cried Woolf

For more than two centuries, adults raised alarms about young people and reading.

Until 1971, no national test tracked whether they were right.

Thirteen-year-olds' average reading score peaked in 2012.

By 2025 it was 256. In 1971 it had been 255.

Scores are on NAEP's 0–500 scale; the chart shows 250 to 265.

Germany's “Lesesucht,” or reading mania, was a 1790s worry about too much novel reading. Rudolf Flesch's Why Johnny Can't Read came out in 1955. A Nation at Risk warned of “a rising tide of mediocrity” in 1983. PISA's 2025 results, released in September 2026, put the U.S. reading score at 490, the lowest in about 25 years; the U.S. sample missed participation standards.

Sources: NAGB, CSBA, Chalkbeat, Education Week on PISA, The Atlantic. Years between 1975 and 2008 come from NAEP's published series.

In 1925, Virginia Woolf called a book of her essays The Common Reader. She meant someone who read for pleasure.

A year later she gave a talk at a girls' school in Kent. It was called “How Should One Read a Book?”

14 of every 100 thirteen-year-olds read for fun almost every day in 2025.

Woolf took the title from Samuel Johnson's Life of Gray. Her common reader read “for his own pleasure,” not to teach or to correct anyone. The 1926 talk was published that year in The Yale Review and, revised, closed her second Common Reader in 1932.

Sources: The Common Reader (Project Gutenberg), Britannica, Smith College, The Yale Review.

Most Children Left Behind

No Child Left Behind became law in January 2002.

In 2024, 40 of every 100 fourth graders scored below Basic in reading, the most since 2002.

40 of 100

Below Basic: 34% in 2019, 37% in 2022, 40% in 2024. Proficient or above: 35%, 33%, 31%.

Below Basic did not mean a child couldn't read at all, Chalkbeat noted. The National Center for Education Statistics advised reading NAEP's achievement levels with caution.

The share at Advanced was higher than in 1992. In 2024, 77% of fourth graders from the wealthiest families scored above the national average.

Sources: NAEP 2024, NAGB, NPR, Chalkbeat.

Below Basic meant a fourth grader likely couldn't recognize the reason behind a character's action when the story only implied it.

Source: National Assessment Governing Board

      Which plan cost Jamie less that year?

      Plan A$2,160
      Plan B$2,520

      Plan A: 12 × $150 + 6 × $60 = $2,160. Plan B: 12 × $110 + 6 × $200 = $2,520. Six visits at $200 came to $1,200, under the $1,500 deductible, so the $20 price never applied.

      At Level 1 or below, the OECD's lowest levels, adults could read short texts and lists with clearly marked information. Comparing sources or finding information spread across a longer text was where they struggled. The U.S. share at that level rose from 19% in 2017 to 28% in 2023.
